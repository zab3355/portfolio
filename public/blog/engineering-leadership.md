I never set out to become a technical lead.

At NBCUniversal I mostly kept stepping in when something needed an owner. That started with helping stabilize a platform, then guiding one team, and eventually supporting several teams working on systems used for live broadcast operations and media asset management.

Over time my role changed. I still write code and work on architecture, but a lot of my job now is making sure the systems and the engineers building them can keep scaling.

Looking back, the transition felt less like a promotion and more like a gradual change in how I spent my time.

## It Doesn’t Feel Like a Promotion

When your responsibilities begin shifting toward leadership, it rarely happens overnight.

You are still solving technical problems, reviewing code, and shipping features. At the same time people begin asking you different questions. They want to know about timelines, technical direction, or how your team will support a new initiative.

For a while it feels like you are doing your old job while slowly taking on a new one.

The biggest shift for me was realizing my value was no longer about being the fastest person to solve a problem.

Instead, my job became helping the people around me solve problems more effectively.

When your focus is purely building features, productivity usually means shipping something yourself. When you start guiding a team, productivity looks different. It might mean helping someone work through a tricky bug, clarifying an architectural decision, or removing a blocker so someone else can finish their work.

The impact spreads across the team instead of staying with one person.

## The Hardest Part: Context Switching

One challenge I did not expect was how often the job switches contexts.

On a typical day I might go from

- reviewing a database migration  
- running sprint planning with a team  
- investigating a production issue  
- discussing system reliability with leadership  
- helping an engineer debug something confusing  

Each situation requires a different way of thinking.

Eventually I realized a big part of the role is connecting different groups.

Engineers need clear technical direction.  
Leadership needs confidence that systems are stable and scalable.

Helping both sides stay aligned becomes part of the work.

## Staying Close to the Technical Work

Some people assume that moving into leadership means stepping away from the technical details.

In my experience the opposite has been true.

Some of the most important decisions still require deep understanding of the systems. During a large migration of media assets connected to a corporate spinoff, we had to move hundreds of thousands of records safely without disrupting production workflows.

That required thinking carefully about

- how migration scripts would interact with the production database  
- what failure scenarios could happen  
- how rollback plans would work if something broke during the process  

These decisions are difficult to make without understanding the technical details.

It also matters for the engineers you work with. Feedback carries more weight when people know you understand the systems they are building.

Because of that I still spend time reviewing code, digging into architecture problems, and helping troubleshoot issues that show up in production.

## Mentorship Became One of the Best Parts

One part of the role I did not expect to enjoy as much as I do is mentorship.

As our teams grew I started helping interview engineers and mentoring people as they learned our systems. Watching someone grow into a stronger developer can be surprisingly rewarding.

One thing I learned quickly is that it is usually better not to jump straight to the answer.

When someone asks for help debugging something, the easiest response is explaining how you would solve it. That approach rarely helps someone build their own problem solving skills.

Instead I ask questions such as

- What have you already ruled out  
- What do you think the system is doing right now  
- Where would you start investigating  

Talking through the problem often helps people arrive at the answer themselves, and that lesson tends to stick.

## Things I Wish I Understood Earlier

A few lessons took me longer than they probably should have.

### Write Things Down

When you are juggling multiple systems and teams, details eventually slip through the cracks.

Architecture decisions, tradeoffs, and context are easy to lose if they only exist in someone’s memory. Writing those things down helps everyone understand how and why a system works the way it does.

### Production Issues Teach You the Most

Working on systems that support live broadcast means reliability matters. When something breaks in production you quickly see how different parts of the system interact.

Those moments can be stressful, but they are also when you gain the clearest understanding of how your architecture actually behaves.

### The Most Valuable Work Is Often Invisible

Some of the most important work never appears in sprint metrics.

It might be

- an architectural decision that prevents a future outage  
- a conversation that clears up confusion between teams  
- time spent helping someone grow as an engineer  

A lot of leadership impact comes from problems that never happen because the right decision was made earlier.

Recognizing that took me a while.
